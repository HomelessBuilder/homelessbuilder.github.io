Interlude: The Hand That Wove Them Together

Chronologically this sits between Part Two and Part Three: the material below explains how the Song of Deborah, the Ark Narrative, and the Court History, already read in those two parts as separate old sources, came to be woven into one continuous history running from the conquest of Canaan through the fall of both kingdoms. Two live scholarly proposals exist for when and how that weaving happened, a single exilic editor working around the 560s BCE, or two editions, an earlier one under King Josiah later revised by a second, exilic hand once his reform failed to prevent the catastrophe it was meant to avert. Both are presented here, neither resolved in favor of the other.

A priest cleaning out a storeroom during repairs to the temple, in the eighteenth year of a young king determined to undo two generations of his grandfather's compromises, found a scroll he did not expect to find at all, and sent it up to the king's own secretary to be read aloud in the palace. Josiah tore his clothes when he heard it. Whatever the scroll actually said, close kin to the law this project has already followed through the wilderness and into Canaan, it said it clearly enough that the king understood immediately how far short of it his own kingdom had fallen, and sent his officials, not to any of the era's more famous prophets, but to a woman named Huldah, to ask whether the scroll's warnings could still be turned aside.

She told them that they could not, not for the kingdom as a whole, judgment for generations of unfaithfulness was already fixed and coming. But because Josiah's own heart had been tender, and he had humbled himself at the scroll's words, he personally would be gathered to his fathers in peace before he had to see the disaster with his own eyes. Josiah took the answer, half mercy and half warning, and used what time it left him well: high places torn down across the whole kingdom, altars to other gods smashed, worship centralized at the Jerusalem temple the scroll itself demanded, a Passover kept with a completeness the record says had not been matched since the very earliest days of the judges, long before there had ever been a king at all.

Someone in that reforming court, a scribe working in the reform's own service, took up the task of arranging the older material already circulating, the poem about a woman named Deborah and another about a woman named Jael, the stories of a captured Ark and a doomed household at Shiloh, the long, unflinching account of David's own household coming apart from the inside, and set it all in order, one continuous argument running from a promised land first entered through to a monarchy Josiah himself now sat at the end of. The pattern the scribe found, arranging it, was not hard to see once laid end to end. In the judges' own era, apostasy brought oppression, oppression brought a cry for help, and the cry brought deliverance, over and over, the same wheel turning through Deborah's generation and every one after it. Once a king sat on the throne, that wheel stopped turning. What replaced it was a slower, straighter decline, broken only rarely by a faithful reformer, a Hezekiah, and now, the scribe had every reason to hope, a Josiah, whose faithfulness might yet be enough to bend the whole downward line back toward something survivable.

It was not enough. Josiah rode out years later to meet an Egyptian army passing through his territory on its way to a war that had nothing to do with him, and was killed at Megiddo, on almost the same stretch of ground where Deborah's own poem had once described chariots bogging down in the mud, though this time no flood came to save the king who died there. The reform he had staked his whole reign on outlived him by less than a generation. Babylon finished what Assyria had started, the temple he had purified was burned, and the very history that had once been arranged to hope for exactly this kind of king now had to be reopened and rewritten by someone standing on the other side of the catastrophe that king's own faithfulness had failed to prevent.

The hand that took the scroll up again, whether the same scribe grown old or a different one entirely, faced a harder question than the first version had ever needed to answer. Not why should we reform, but why did reforming not save us. The answer this later editing settled on reached backward past Josiah entirely, to his own grandfather Manasseh, whose reign the record blames without qualification for sins so total that the Lord would not turn from the fierceness of his great wrath, kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him. Josiah's own faithfulness, on this accounting, had bought his own generation a reprieve and nothing more. What his grandfather had set in motion could be delayed by one righteous king's whole reign, but not undone by it.

The history closes, in the form that reached us, on a small, strange note neither despair nor triumph. Decades into exile, the Babylonian king who had once conquered Jerusalem died, and his successor released the long-imprisoned Judean king Jehoiachin from the prison he had sat in for over three and a half decades, spoke kindly to him, gave him a seat above the other exiled kings at that same court, and let him eat at the king's own table for the rest of his life. It is the last thing the whole history reports, and it settles nothing. It is not restoration. It is not a return to the land, or the throne, or anything the earlier chapters had once hoped a faithful king's reform might yet secure. It is only a captive treated, at the very end, with more kindness than the years before it had led anyone to expect, and the editor who chose to close the whole sweeping account there, rather than anywhere else, left it deliberately unclear whether that kindness was the first sign of something larger still to come, or simply the last small mercy history had left to record.

What this project's own account of Deborah, and Jael, and the Ark, and David's ruined household never made explicit, reading them each in their own moment, is what this later hand's arrangement makes plain: none of it had been kept, copied, and finally bound into one continuous history merely because it happened. It had been selected, and set in order, and given a shape, by someone standing in the wreckage of a monarchy the whole account had been arranged, more than once, to try to save.
