Part Six: The Same War, Told Four Ways

Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Ethiopian tradition hold this era's books as scripture; Protestant and Jewish canons don't. Judith's historical framing is deliberately garbled, a signal of didactic fiction rather than claimed record, the same way Job's setting outside Israel was. 2 Maccabees survives as an epitome of a longer, lost work by a diaspora historian from Cyrene, in North Africa; the fuller original doesn't survive to check the abridgment against.

I. The Fish and the Debt

Tobit had gone blind the ordinary way a man in Nineveh might go blind, sparrows nesting above him in the courtyard wall while he slept off a day spent burying an executed man's body against the king's own decree, their droppings falling into his open eyes and leaving a white film neither he nor any physician he paid could clear afterward. He had done a dangerous, decent thing, and the price of it turned out to be his sight, which was not the kind of arithmetic Tobit's own faith had ever promised him, and he kept the faith anyway.

Before sending his son Tobias to collect an old debt in Media, he gave him instruction that ran longer than the errand itself required: give alms as you're able, don't withhold your bread from the hungry, and never do to anyone what you yourself would hate to have done to you. Tobias set out with a hired companion, a young man calling himself Azarias who knew the roads better than anyone had a right to, and who was, though nobody in the story except the reader is told this outright until the end, the angel Raphael traveling in a stranger's shape.

Something enormous rose out of the Tigris and tried to swallow Tobias's foot on the first night's camp. Azarias told him to catch it instead, and to keep the heart, the liver, and the gall, which was strange instruction for a young man to trust from a stranger, and Tobias trusted it anyway. It mattered soon after, in Ecbatana, where Tobias fell in love with a kinswoman named Sarah who had already been married seven times, each husband dead in his own bridal chamber before the marriage could be completed, killed, the household believed, by a demon jealous of her. On their own wedding night Tobias burned the fish's heart and liver on the coals as instructed, and the smell of it drove the demon out of the room entirely, out of the country, as far as it could get, until it was bound at the far edge of Egypt and troubled no one again.

They came home together, and the fish's gall, rubbed into Tobit's clouded eyes, peeled the white film away and gave him his sight back, quietly and without ceremony, the way the whole account had run: not a single visible miracle from beginning to end, only a young man's obedience to strange instructions, a woman's survival of a horror none of it was her fault, and a father's blindness undone by the same ordinary-seeming care that had cost him his sight burying a stranger in the first place. Only at the very end did Azarias tell them who he actually was, and refuse any payment for the road, and go back up the way he'd come. God, in this account, had never once acted directly in front of anyone. He had simply arranged, patiently and from a distance, for a fish, a debt, and an unlucky young woman to intersect with a family that kept faith in the dark.

II. What the Grandson Carried to Egypt

Ben Sira kept a house of instruction in Jerusalem where young men came to learn what he had spent a long life collecting: how to speak, how to eat at a rich man's table without losing your dignity, how to grieve properly and stop grieving on schedule, how wisdom and the law Moses had brought down from Sinai were, in the end, not two things but one. All this, he told his students, pointing to the very scroll of the covenant, is the book of wisdom itself, not a separate teaching alongside it. He had watched the high priest Simon come out from behind the sanctuary veil on the great feast days, robed for the ceremony, and had never found language too extravagant to describe it: like the sun shining on the temple, he wrote, like the rainbow gleaming in the clouds, like a lily by a spring of water. Wisdom, worship, and the concrete daily order of the temple's own establishment were, in Ben Sira's teaching, never separated from each other at all.

His grandson left Jerusalem decades later and settled in Egypt, in a Jewish community that had been Greek-speaking for generations and had, by then, largely lost easy access to Hebrew. He spent years there turning his grandfather's teaching into Greek a diaspora household could actually read to its own children, and when he finished, he wrote a short account of his own reasons for the work, dating it by the regnal year of the Ptolemaic king ruling in the city where he'd made his home. Much, he admitted in that same short preface, is lost in translation, a thing said with real force in one language rarely lands with the same force in another, and he asked his readers' patience for whatever had slipped in the crossing. What he was actually doing, in that admission, was something larger than apology: proving, by the plain existence of the work itself, that a Jewish grandfather's wisdom teaching in Jerusalem and a Jewish grandson's careful labor in Egypt belonged to one continuous project, not two separate stories that happened to share a bloodline.

III. Two Books, One War

At Modein, an old priest named Mattathias watched a king's officer set up an altar in the town square and order the people forward to offer the sacrifice the king now required of them on pain of death. When a Jewish man stepped up first, willing to comply, Mattathias killed him where he stood at the altar, killed the king's officer beside him, tore the altar down with his own hands, and ran for the hills with his sons, calling out as he went for everyone who still cared for the law and the covenant to follow him. Men did.

What followed, in the sober telling, was a war fought and mostly won: Judas Maccabeus leading raids and pitched battles against forces that badly outnumbered him, the temple recovered and its altar rebuilt and rededicated with an eight-day festival of thanksgiving, and, over the years after Judas's own death in battle, his brothers Jonathan and then Simon consolidating what he had started into something close to an independent Jewish state again, the first in centuries. This account gave the credit outright to the men who did the fighting and the governing, with little of the miraculous laid over the top of it, closer in style to the old court histories of David's own reign than to anything visionary.

A different telling of the identical years, condensed down from a much longer work by a historian named Jason, writing from Cyrene on the North African coast, kept the same events but changed almost everything about how they were meant to land on a reader. Its centerpiece was not a battle at all. A mother and her seven sons were brought before the king together and ordered, one at a time, to eat pork in violation of the law, and refused, one at a time, while the others were made to watch what happened to the one before them. The eldest spoke first and died for it. The second, dying, told the king that the King of the universe would raise them all up to an everlasting renewal of life, since they were dying for his laws. Each brother in turn said some version of the same thing, refusing the food, refusing the king's growing fury, until only the youngest and the mother were left. She had watched six sons killed in front of her by then, and when the king offered to spare the last one if she would only persuade him, she leaned close instead and told him, in their own language so the king's men beside her couldn't follow it, not to be afraid, to take his place among his brothers, and to trust that the same mercy that had made him would give him back to her again. He refused as his brothers had. She was killed last of all.

The two accounts cover the same king, the same persecution, very nearly the same years. One remembers it as a war fought and won by capable men. The other remembers it as seven executions and a mother's courage, told by a diaspora scholar in North Africa who never saw the temple himself and chose, from that distance, to preserve the part of the story that no army's victory could have supplied on its own.

IV. The Widow's Hand

Bethulia had held out under siege long enough that its elders were ready to surrender on the fifth day if no relief came, and Judith, a wealthy widow who had kept strict mourning and fasting since her husband's death, went before the town council and told them that setting a deadline on God's mercy was itself the sin, not the siege. Then she did something about it herself. She dressed carefully, for the first time in years, and walked out through the gate toward the enemy camp with a single servant and a bag of her own kosher food, and let herself be brought before the general Holofernes as a woman fleeing a doomed town with useful intelligence to trade.

She stayed in his camp several days, keeping her own dietary law scrupulously even there, praying at night outside the camp under pretense of ritual purification, and letting the general grow more confident of her, and more drawn to her, with every evening that passed. On the fourth night he held a private banquet for her alone and drank himself into a stupor no general facing a siege should ever have allowed himself. When the tent was empty but for the two of them, she took his own sword down from where it hung by the bed, and struck twice, and carried his head back to Bethulia in the food bag she'd brought her own provisions in from the start.

It was not the first time a woman with no army behind her had ended a powerful man inside his own tent, and the better part of a thousand years had passed since the last one. Whether Judith herself, devout and well versed in her own people's scripture, knew exactly whose example she was standing in when she took up that sword, the account doesn't say. It doesn't need to. The story's own first hearers would have heard the echo without any help at all, and understood, the same way they'd understood it about Jael, that deliverance in this tradition kept arriving from the hand least expected to carry it, and kept costing the woman who carried it something real to do it.

V. Alexandria's Answer

A different teacher, generations after Ben Sira's grandson had made his own crossing into Egypt, wrote in Solomon's borrowed name too, the way it had become an accepted convention for wisdom writers to do when they wanted the weight of the old king's name behind an argument the historical Solomon himself, who never spoke a word of Greek in his life, could not actually have made. This writer praised Wisdom as present at creation itself, argued that the righteous soul does not simply end at death, using categories his own Greek-educated neighbors in Alexandria would have recognized from their own philosophers, mocked the carved idols around him with real contempt, and retold the story of the Exodus as an extended meditation on the exact justice by which Egypt's own punishments had answered Egypt's own crimes.

Two centuries earlier, a family under the same kind of pressure from the same spreading Greek culture had answered it by drawing the boundary tighter, insisting Abraham himself had already kept the whole law before Sinai so that nothing in their present resistance could be called an innovation. This teacher, facing the identical pressure in a Greek-speaking city far from Jerusalem, answered it the opposite way: walking further into Greek thought than almost any Jewish writer before him had dared, and holding on, the whole way through, to the core conviction that had sent him there in the first place. Separation and synthesis: a family in Judea and a scholar in Alexandria, answering the same threat two centuries and a sea apart, each of them a serious response the other's existence doesn't diminish.

A shorter work, gathered rather than composed by one hand, circulated under yet another borrowed name from the worst of the exile years, Jeremiah's own scribe Baruch, though the scribe himself had been dead a long time before any of this particular text existed. It was less a new argument than an anthology of griefs and hopes the tradition had already produced, a confession of the exile's own guilt, a hymn to Wisdom, and a vision of Jerusalem herself, personified as a grieving mother, comforted at last with the promise that the children scattered from her would be gathered home. A scribe's name from the darkest years still carried enough weight, a century and more later, that a later community reached for it to carry their own later hope.

A few shorter pieces round out what the different traditions kept from this same crowded stretch of centuries, and none of them sit the same way twice across Catholic, Orthodox, and Ethiopian practice: an extra psalm in David's own first-person voice about killing Goliath, a penitential prayer supplying words 2 Chronicles had said a captive king once spoke without ever writing them down, and a Greek retelling of the return from exile that adds, almost as an aside, a court contest over what is strongest in the world, wine, the king, or women, until a young guardsman wins the whole argument by naming something none of the others had thought to say. Truth, he told the assembled court, is strongest of all, and endures, and lives, and prevails forever. He was rewarded for the answer. Whether the story is remembered as scripture or simply as a good tale told once at a Persian court depends, in the end, on which tradition happens to be doing the remembering.
