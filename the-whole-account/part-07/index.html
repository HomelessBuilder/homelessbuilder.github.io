Part Seven: Letters Before Any Gospel

Seven letters carry near-universal scholarly consensus as genuinely Paul's own: 1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon. All predate Mark, the earliest Gospel, by roughly fifteen to twenty years. Philippians and Philemon are placed last here, following the source's own sequence, though that placement is explicitly provisional: if Paul wrote them from an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus rather than a later one at Rome, both letters would actually belong alongside 1 and 2 Corinthians in the early-to-mid 50s. This project does not resolve which is correct.

I. Those Who Sleep

Timothy came back to Paul in Corinth with news that was mostly good and partly not. The church at Thessalonica, planted in a matter of weeks before opposition forced Paul out of the city, was still standing, still holding to what it had been taught, and still, visibly, afraid of something Paul had not expected to have to address so soon: some of their own had already died, ordinary deaths, before the return of Christ they had all been taught to expect within their own lifetimes, and the survivors did not know what that meant for the ones now in the ground. Would the dead have missed it. Would they be left behind, somehow, by believers still living when the day finally came.

Paul answered the fear directly, without softening how odd it must have sounded to raise it that openly. We do not want you to be uninformed about those who have fallen asleep, he wrote, so that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope. The dead in Christ would rise first, not last, not disadvantaged in any way by having died early, and only then would the living be gathered together with them. It was pastoral reassurance built on something Paul treated as already established teaching rather than a new claim he was introducing to comfort them, a resurrection hope circulating in the movement long before anyone had written any of it down.

He spent the earlier part of the same letter defending his own conduct during that brief founding visit, against some suggestion that he'd come only for money, or flattery, or his own advantage, the kind of accusation any traveling teacher in that world had reason to expect and needed to answer directly if he wanted to be trusted again. You know, and God knows, he told them, how devoutly and uprightly and blamelessly we behaved. It was a small, human defense, sitting inside what would turn out to be the oldest surviving piece of Christian writing anywhere, composed a full generation before anyone thought to write down a connected account of Jesus's own life at all. The earliest thing the movement left behind was not a story about him. It was a letter about grief, and who gets left out of hope, and who doesn't.

II. The Face at Antioch

The letter that went out to the churches in Galatia carried no opening thanks at all, which was itself a message before a single argument had been made: Paul was too angry to observe the courtesy. I am astonished, he wrote, that you are so quickly deserting the one who called you, for a different gospel entirely, and there is no other, only some who are troubling you and wanting to distort the gospel of Christ. Men had come after Paul's own visit and told his Gentile converts that faith in Christ was not, by itself, enough. They needed circumcision too, and the full weight of the law besides, if they wanted to actually belong.

Paul answered by telling his own story first: called directly, he insisted, not taught by any human source, independent of Jerusalem's authority long enough that when he finally did go up to meet James and Peter and John, it was to have what he was already preaching confirmed, not corrected. They saw that I had been entrusted with the gospel to the uncircumcised, he wrote, and gave me the right hand of fellowship. It should have settled the matter. It didn't, and the place it came apart in front of everyone was Antioch, not Galatia at all, a scene Paul recounted in this letter because the Galatians needed to understand exactly how high the stakes of this same argument had already run once before.

Peter had been eating freely with Gentile believers in Antioch, at the same table, no separate seating, no separate menu, a plain physical fact that carried enormous social weight in that world: who you ate with was who you counted as one of your own. Then certain men came from James, and Peter drew back and kept himself separate, afraid, Paul says, of the circumcision party, and the rest of the Jewish believers followed him, even Barnabas, Paul's own longtime partner in the work. Paul opposed him to his face, in front of the whole congregation, because he stood condemned. What Peter was eating had never been the point. What table he was willing to be seen at, and who he was willing to be seen sharing it with, was the entire argument, played out in a single meal in front of a watching congregation that had to decide, right then, whose example to follow.

The letter's central claim, that a person is justified by faith and not by works of the law, has been read for centuries as a broader argument about earning salvation through effort of any kind, a reading that still has serious defenders. A substantial and growing body of more recent scholarship argues instead that Paul's own target, in his own moment, was narrower and sharper than that: whether Gentile believers had to take on Jewish ethnic identity markers, circumcision most of all, to be fully inside the community at all. Neither reading has driven the other from the field, and this project doesn't pretend the question is closed. What can be said with more confidence is only that the narrower version of that argument had already been made physically visible to everyone sitting at Antioch's dinner table, whatever the letter's fuller theological reach turns out to mean beyond that one meal.

III. What Love Actually Regulates, and What Was Handed Down

The church at Corinth Paul had planted was, by the time this letter reached them, coming apart along more seams than one. Some claimed loyalty to Paul, others to Apollos, others to Peter, others insisted they needed no human teacher at all and answered only to Christ directly, a factionalism dividing the very people who were supposed to share one table and one loaf. Members were dragging each other into pagan courts over property disputes. A man was living with his father's wife and the congregation, instead of troubled by it, seemed almost proud of how broad-minded that tolerance made them look. In worship itself, people competed openly over which spiritual gift carried more prestige, some who spoke in ecstatic tongues looking down on those who didn't, a status contest breaking out in the middle of what was supposed to be shared worship.

It was into that specific rivalry, not into a wedding, that Paul wrote the passage that would eventually be read at more weddings than any other text in the letter. If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal. Whatever gift a Corinthian believer was most proud of, prophecy, knowledge, tongues, faith enough to move mountains, none of it counted for anything set against love, and love itself, he told them, does not envy, does not boast, is not arrogant, does not insist on its own way, precisely the behaviors currently splitting the congregation apart over whose gift outranked whose.

He reminded them too of what he had handed on to them at the very start, in language he was careful to mark as something he had received rather than composed: for I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus, on the night when he was betrayed, took bread. It was the earliest written account of that meal anywhere, older than any Gospel's version of the same night, and Paul framed it explicitly as inherited, fixed, already treasured before it ever reached his own hand to pass along.

He did the same with the resurrection, near the letter's end, because some in Corinth had begun denying it outright, treating any bodily resurrection as beneath serious belief. For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received, he wrote, that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared, and then a list, name after name, hundreds of witnesses, most of them, he added, still alive and able to be asked directly. If Christ has not been raised, he told them, your faith is futile. It was not a new argument invented to win a local dispute. It was the oldest fixed formula the movement had, recited to a fractured congregation as the one thing underneath all their factions they still, at bottom, actually shared.

IV. Power Made Perfect in Weakness

Something had gone wrong between the first letter and this one. New teachers had arrived in Corinth since, well-credentialed, confident public speakers, presenting themselves with exactly the polish the Greek city's own culture prized, and measured against them, Paul knew exactly how he looked: his bodily presence is weak, some were already saying of him, and his speech of no account. He did not try to out-credential them. He did something a status-conscious Mediterranean audience would have found genuinely strange, and told them so himself, that it would sound like boasting, and then catalogued, in detail, every mark against him: five times whipped by his own people, beaten with rods three times, stoned once, shipwrecked three times, a night and a day adrift at sea, danger from rivers, from robbers, from his own people and from Gentiles, hunger, cold, sleepless nights, and beyond all of it, the daily pressure of his anxiety for every church he'd planted.

He mentioned, almost sideways, a private experience years earlier, a man in Christ, he called it, careful not to claim it outright for himself even though no reader missed who he meant, caught up into the third heaven and shown things no human speech was permitted to repeat. And to keep him from becoming too elated by it, he told them, he had been given a thorn in the flesh, never named more specifically than that, and had asked three times to have it removed, and been refused, and told instead: my grace is sufficient for you, my power is made perfect in weakness.

It was the opposite argument from anything the super-apostles were making about themselves, and Paul knew it, and made it anyway, on purpose: for when I am weak, then I am strong. He asked them, in the same letter, to finish a collection they'd started for the poor believers back in Jerusalem, a concrete transfer of money from Gentile congregations to a Jewish mother church hundreds of miles away, binding communities together materially in a way no argument about doctrine alone could have managed. A church being courted by men who looked the part of legitimate authority was being asked, instead, to trust a man whose only offered credential was everything that world would ordinarily have counted against him.

V. All Israel, In the End

Paul wrote to Rome without ever having stood in front of the church he was writing to, which changed the letter itself: no local dispute to referee, no factional gossip to untangle, only the fullest and most careful account of his own gospel he ever put on paper, prepared for a church he hoped would help send him further west, to Spain, once he'd finally made it there in person.

He began where he began everywhere else he made this argument in full, with the plain claim that no one, Jew or Gentile, stood innocent before God on their own merits, and moved from there to Abraham, reaching back past Sinai entirely to a man who had been counted righteous by faith before any law existed to keep or break, the same man an old promise had once named the one through whom every family on earth would be blessed. Union with Christ, Paul argued, ran through baptism the way death and burial ran into rising again, a pattern enacted in water on every new believer's own body.

He did not pretend the life this demanded was easy to hold to. I do not do the good I want, he wrote, but the evil I do not want is what I keep doing, a confession of a divided will any reader who had ever actually tried to live by conviction would recognize immediately, and against that struggle he set the sweep of the chapter that followed: no condemnation now for those in Christ, the whole of creation itself groaning toward its own liberation, nothing in life or death able to separate anyone from the love he was describing.

What he could not resolve, and did not pretend to, was the question sitting underneath the whole letter: why so many of his own people, the people this promise had first been given to, had not accepted it. He wrestled with it across three long chapters rather than past it, grief he called it, and unceasing anguish in my heart, refusing every easy answer that would have let him close the question quickly. What he landed on, in the end, was not a system. A partial hardening has come upon Israel, he wrote, until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in, and in that way all Israel will be saved, by a mechanism he was honest enough to call, in the very next line, a depth he had no full account of himself. Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God. How unsearchable are his judgments, and how inscrutable his ways. It was the most theologically ambitious letter he ever wrote, and it ended, at its hardest question, in something closer to worship than to an argument won.

VI. The Word From Prison

Wherever exactly he was writing from, chained, guarded, uncertain how the case against him would end, Paul's letter to Philippi carried almost none of the strain visible in his letter to Corinth. This was a church that had loved him without complication from the start, that had sent one of their own, a man named Epaphroditus, to bring him material support and stay to help however he could. Epaphroditus had fallen seriously ill not long after arriving, close enough to death that word of it had already reached Philippi and worried them badly, and Paul, moved as much by their anxiety as by the man's own recovery, sent him home early with the letter itself, eager to put their fear to rest before it had chance to fester further.

Inside that warmth sat a passage some scholars believe Paul did not compose at all, but was quoting, an older hymn already sung in the earliest years of the movement, describing Christ as existing in the form of God and not counting equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptying himself, taking the form of a servant, humbling himself to death, even death on a cross, and for that reason exalted, given the name above every name, so that every knee should bow. If it really was older than this letter, sung and recited before Paul ever wrote a word of scripture down himself, it meant a community only a decade or two removed from the crucifixion was already singing, in fixed liturgical language, about a pre-existent, self-emptying, exalted Christ, long before any of it appeared in a Gospel's own narrative form.

The shorter letter he wrote from the same imprisonment, to a man named Philemon, carried none of that theological reach and all of the letter's own hard, practical weight instead. Onesimus, Philemon's slave, had run, possibly with something stolen besides, and had found his way to Paul, and become, during Paul's own confinement, a believer, useful to him now in ways his name, which meant useful, made almost too pointed to miss. Paul sent him back, which the law of that world required, but sent him back carrying a letter that asked Philemon to receive him no longer as a slave, but as more than a slave, a beloved brother, and offered to cover personally whatever debt or theft stood between them.

He did not command Philemon to free him. He said only that he was confident of Philemon's obedience, knowing he would do even more than what was asked, language careful enough to leave the actual decision, and the actual cost of it, entirely in the hands of a man who owned another human being and was now being asked, by a prisoner with no power to enforce anything, to reconsider what that ownership meant. Whether Philemon did what was asked of him, whether Onesimus came home a brother or stayed exactly what he had been before he ran, is not a question this short letter, or any other record from that world, ever answers. It ends mid-request, the reply unrecorded, the way a great deal of what this movement asked of the people already living inside its world's own structures went unrecorded too.
