Part Nine: A Movement Learning to Wait

Of the thirteen letters gathered here, only a minority have a strong case for direct authorship by the names attached to them. Writing in a revered teacher's name after his death was a real, accepted ancient convention, not automatically deception in the modern sense; this project doesn't resolve the ethics of the practice, only reports where the evidence points letter by letter. James's own date is unusually wide open, anywhere from the 40s to the mid-second century, and is left open here rather than assigned.

I. Faith That Does Something

A poor man in a shabby coat and a rich man with gold rings and fine clothes walked into the same assembly on the same morning, in the small scene this letter uses to make its whole argument in miniature, and the rich man was shown to the good seat while the poor man was told to stand, or sit on the floor by someone's feet, if he insisted on staying at all. Have you not then made distinctions among yourselves, the letter asked, and become judges with evil thoughts.

It went further than a rebuke of manners. Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you, it said, naming names no one in that assembly could pretend not to recognize: wages you kept back from the laborers who mowed your fields are crying out, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord. It was the same complaint a shepherd from Tekoa had once carried into a northern king's own royal sanctuary two centuries before this letter was written, justice denied to the poor while the festivals ran on schedule, now carried into a very different assembly by a very different kind of author, and landing with exactly the same force.

The letter's most quoted line ran directly against another argument this project has already followed in full. Faith without works is dead, it insisted, and pointed to Abraham himself, the same man an earlier writer had held up as proof that faith alone, apart from any law, was enough to be counted righteous. Was not Abraham justified by works, this letter asked instead, when he offered up his son Isaac on the altar. Whether the two accounts genuinely contradicted each other, or simply meant something different by the same word, works, one author picturing a Gentile forced to adopt Jewish identity markers, the other picturing plain ethical action of any kind toward the suffering poor in front of him, is not a question either writer stopped to settle for the other's benefit. Both landed inside the same growing collection of letters anyway, arguing past each other, and neither writer so much as blinked.

II. The Body and the Household

A teaching had taken root in one small congregation that braided together strict ascetic discipline, a certain reverence toward angelic powers, and a confidence that mastering the right disciplines gave a person real spiritual advantage over everyone else in the room. The letter answering it did not argue point by point so much as overwhelm the whole premise: in Christ, it said, the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily, and you have been filled in him, who is the head of all rule and authority. Whatever partial powers or private disciplines this teaching was promising, the letter's answer was simply that nothing partial was left to chase after any longer.

A related letter, less tied to any one local dispute, widened the same claim into something closer to a vision of the whole community itself. He is our peace, it said, who has made us both one, and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility, the very wall this project has watched built and rebuilt, generation after generation, since a returning community stood trembling in the rain deciding which marriages to end to keep itself intact. This letter claimed, flatly, that the wall was already down, not a live fight still being fought but an accomplished fact the community was simply being told to live inside.

Both letters carried, further in, a household code laying out obligations between husbands and wives, parents and children, and masters and the people they owned outright. Slaves, obey your earthly masters, one of them said, and in the very next breath, masters, treat your slaves justly and fairly, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven. It asked something real of the men who held that power, an obligation owed even to people the law itself treated as property, and it did not, in the same breath, ask them to stop owning anyone at all. A single letter to a single slaveholder, written by the same hand or one very like it, had once asked a man to receive a runaway back as a brother rather than property, and not said outright that the receiving required setting him free. This later, more general instruction did not go further than that letter had. Whether these later words came from Paul's own hand, developing his thinking in a final stretch of his life, or from a community writing in his name to carry his authority into questions he had never lived to face directly, remains, honestly, unresolved.

III. The Day That Would Not Come

Word had reached one young church, apparently carrying Paul's own name behind it though he had not actually written it, that the day of the Lord had already arrived and simply passed them by unnoticed. A letter went out correcting the claim directly: that day would not come, it said, until a rebellion came first, and a figure it called the man of lawlessness was revealed, currently held back by something the letter never named clearly enough for later readers to identify with any confidence. Some in the community, apparently certain the end was close enough that ordinary labor no longer mattered, had stopped working altogether. If anyone is not willing to work, the letter told them bluntly, let him not eat. It sat in real, unresolved tension with an earlier letter to this same city, one insisting the day would come suddenly, like a thief in the night, exactly the kind of unpredictable arrival this newer letter was now working to slow down and postpone. Whether the same hand wrote both, correcting an urgency it had itself once encouraged, or whether a later voice was gently walking back an expectation events had simply outlived, isn't a question this project resolves either.

A different letter reached scattered churches across Asia Minor, none of them Paul's own communities, facing hardship whose exact shape, official persecution or simply local suspicion and social exclusion, this project can't confidently specify either. What it offered them was neither urgency about an ending nor institutional correction, but a reframing of the suffering itself: you share Christ's own sufferings, it told them, so that you may also rejoice and be glad when his glory is revealed. It carried a household code of its own, close in shape to the one already read, and one passage stranger and more debated than almost anything else in this whole collection, a claim that Christ, in the time between his death and resurrection, went and proclaimed something to spirits now in prison, disobedient long ago in Noah's own day. What that claim actually meant to its first hearers has never been settled with any real confidence, then or since, and this project doesn't pretend otherwise.

IV. What Went Out From Us

A community had split, and the letter written afterward did not soften how it described the break. They went out from us, it said, but they were not of us, for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us. What had driven part of the community out, or driven the rest to push them out, was a specific claim some among them were making: that Jesus had not truly come in the flesh at all, only seemed to, a teaching the letter rejected without any patience for nuance. Every spirit that confesses Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God, it said, and every spirit that does not confess this is not. Set against that hard boundary line, the same letter offered the gentlest, most repeated claim in the whole collection: God is love, and whoever abides in love abides in God, and God abides in him. Two shorter letters followed the same concern into smaller rooms, one warning a specific community not to welcome traveling teachers who taught the same denial, the other airing an entirely local, almost petty dispute over a leader named Diotrephes who refused hospitality to traveling believers and pushed out of the assembly anyone who tried to receive them anyway, a small, human glimpse of ordinary church politics sitting right alongside the collection's weightiest theological claims.

A shorter letter still, dense and urgent, warned against teachers the author considered a corrupting danger to the whole community, and did something none of the other twelve letters in this era did so openly. Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, it said, and quoted, word for word, the vision of judgment this project has already followed across an entire earlier part of its own account: the Lord comes with ten thousand of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all. The letter felt no need to explain who Enoch was, or defend citing him as though the point were even debatable. It simply quoted him as prophecy, the same standing given to anything else called scripture in that world, material an Ethiopian church would go on treasuring as canon for two thousand years while most of the rest of the tradition eventually let it go. The letter mentioned, too, in passing, a dispute between the archangel Michael and the devil over the body of Moses, a story that doesn't survive anywhere in full any longer, referenced here as though everyone listening already knew exactly which story was meant. Whatever else this brief, fierce letter argued, it stood as plain evidence that at least one early Christian author had never drawn the line between scripture and other trusted tradition nearly as firmly as later generations would come to assume he must have.

V. Sound Doctrine, and a Thousand Years Like a Day

A voice carrying Paul's own authority, whether his own late hand or a circle of his followers writing after him, spent three letters instructing two younger men, Timothy and Titus, in something the earlier, more improvised letters had never needed to spend much time on: how to build leadership sturdy enough to outlast the founding generation. An overseer, one letter specified, must be above reproach, the husband of one wife, sober-minded, able to teach, not a recent convert, well thought of by outsiders. Deacons needed their own list of qualifications besides. False teachings were named and warned against with real specificity, and sound doctrine, a phrase this era used more than any before it, was something now to be guarded and handed down intact, not improvised fresh in every new city the way it once had been. A movement that had spent its first decades certain the world was about to end was, by the time these letters were written, quietly organizing itself instead for a future stretching decades further than anyone in Paul's own earliest letters had planned for.

The last of the New Testament's letters to be written, by most reckonings, carried the strongest case for a name attached to it that wasn't its true author's own. It leaned openly on the short, fierce letter just read, using much of its language and its warnings nearly word for word, and it made one admission along the way that told its own reader how much time had passed since Paul first began writing: it spoke of Paul's own letters as an established, gathered collection already, some of it hard to understand, the letter said, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other scriptures, Paul's own words now sitting, in this later writer's own casual phrase, beside scripture itself.

Its real subject, though, was delay, the same delay the very first letter this whole part of the project opened with had never expected to have to explain. Scoffers had begun to ask, openly, where is the promise of his coming, since everything continues as it was from the beginning of creation. The answer offered here was not retreat, and not embarrassment. With the Lord one day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years like one day, it said, and what looked to an impatient reader like failure to arrive was, read this way, no failure at all, only a patience operating on a scale no single human lifetime was built to measure honestly. It was the New Testament's own last word, chronologically, on the very question its earliest surviving letter had first raised about grief and delay and what happens to hope when the thing it was promised keeps not arriving on schedule. The answer had changed very little in all the years between. Wait. It is not what you think it means.
