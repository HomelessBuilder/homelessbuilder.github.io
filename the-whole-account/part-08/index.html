Part Eight: A Generation Later

Mark, likely written around the temple's destruction in 70 CE, is the earliest Gospel by a wide margin; Matthew and Luke both draw on Mark and on a body of shared sayings material no manuscript of which has ever been found, a scholarly reconstruction rather than a discovered text. Mark's own earliest manuscripts end at 16:8; the longer resurrection-appearance ending familiar from most printed Bibles is widely judged a later addition, not part of the original. Luke-Acts' date is genuinely debated, and John is substantially independent of the other three. One scene in this part, the official's baptism, is drawn from Acts 8 but wasn't among the twelve source files this project began from; it's included deliberately, not as an oversight corrected, to give real weight to material this project's own African-Christianity framework exists to recover rather than footnote. The kingdom in that scene is Kush, centered at Meroë in what is now Sudan, whose ruling queens carried the title kandake, rendered "Candace" in Greek; it is a different kingdom from the highland Aksumite state that would adopt Christianity as its own official religion in the fourth century and whose church preserved 1 Enoch and Jubilees in Part Five. Both are real, independent African encounters with the faith, and this project keeps them distinct rather than folding them into one continuous "Ethiopia."

I. The Ending That Would Not Resolve

Whoever wrote the account that would circulate first was writing for people who had just watched the temple burn, the war with Rome finished and lost, a generation of eyewitnesses to everything that came before now old or dead or scattered, and the stories about to go with them if no one set them down. There was no room, in what got written, for a birth. It opened instead with a grown man coming up out of the water at his own baptism, the sky torn open above him, a voice naming him beloved, and moved from there at a pace the writer himself seemed to feel he had no time to slow down: immediately, immediately, one urgent scene crowding into the next.

He healed and cast out unclean spirits and told almost everyone he helped, and the spirits themselves, to keep quiet about who he was, a command repeated so often across the account that the silence itself became a kind of signature, an identity offered and then immediately withdrawn from anyone who might announce it before its own time. He was not gentle by disposition in this telling. He looked around a synagogue at men waiting to see whether he'd break their Sabbath rule by healing a man's withered hand, and the account says he was angry, grieved at their hardness of heart, and healed the man anyway in front of them. His own family came to restrain him at one point, having heard people say he was out of his mind, and when told his mother and brothers were outside asking for him, he looked at the crowd sitting around him instead and said, here are my mother and my brothers.

A full third of the account is given to his last week alone, as though everything before it had only been preparation for what happened in Jerusalem. Darkness came over the whole land at midday while he hung on the cross, and near the end he cried out, in his own language, my God, my God, why have you forsaken me, words the writer left untranslated before rendering their meaning, as though the raw sound mattered as much as the sense. The temple curtain tore in two, top to bottom. And a Roman soldier standing at the foot of the cross, watching a man die who had said nothing grand about himself the entire time this account had followed him, said the one plain thing no follower had yet dared to say aloud: truly this man was God's son. Not a disciple. An outsider, a man paid to carry out executions, delivering the account's first fully unguarded declaration of who this had been.

Women went to the tomb at first light two days later with spices for a body they still expected to find. The stone was already rolled back. A young man in white told them he had risen, and was going ahead of them to Galilee, and to tell the disciples, and Peter especially, and they ran from the tomb instead, trembling, out of their minds with what they'd just been told, and said nothing to anyone, because they were afraid. That is where the account, in its earliest surviving copies, actually ends. No appearance of the risen man himself. No triumphant closing scene. Fear, and silence, and a story the earliest women entrusted with it could not, in that first moment, bring themselves to tell. A different, longer ending was added by a later hand at some point after, giving readers the resurrection appearances this abrupt close withholds. The account as first written did not include it, and did not, on its own terms, seem to think it needed to.

II. A New Moses

A later account opened with a list most modern readers skip past without reading closely, forty-odd names running from Abraham down through David to a carpenter named Joseph, arranged deliberately into three matched sets, because the writer wanted his own community, still working out what it meant to be Jewish and follow this man in the years after the temple's fall, to see the whole of their own scripture's history arriving, name by name, at exactly this point.

Joseph found his betrothed already carrying a child not his own, and considered ending the engagement quietly rather than exposing her to public shame, until a dream told him otherwise: what was conceived in her was of the holy spirit, and he was to name the boy Jesus, for he would save his people from their sins. The writer added, in his own voice, that this fulfilled what had been spoken through the prophet: a virgin shall conceive and bear a son. The word he was reading, in the Greek scripture his own community used, said virgin outright. Centuries earlier, in Hebrew, spoken to a frightened king facing a very different crisis, the same sign had most likely promised something nearer at hand than any of this writer's community had reason to know. He was not lying, or inventing the connection out of nothing. He was reading his own scripture the way his own community had learned to read it, seeing in an old sign's later Greek wording something the sign's first hearers, seven centuries earlier, could not have meant by it, and doing so with complete sincerity.

Wise men came from the east following a star, and Herod, hearing a king had been born, ordered every boy in Bethlehem two years old and under killed rather than risk a rival. The family fled to Egypt and stayed there until Herod died, and the writer, telling it, could not resist the shape of what he was describing: out of Egypt I have called my son, he wrote, quoting an old line originally about the whole nation's exodus centuries before, now folded onto this one family's flight into the same country and back out of it again, Israel's whole story replayed in miniature in one child's infancy.

He grew, in this telling, into a teacher who went up a mountain to deliver his central teaching, deliberately evoking, for anyone who knew their own scripture, another lawgiver who had once gone up a different mountain to bring something down for the same people. Blessed are the poor in spirit, he said, blessed are those who mourn, blessed are the meek, arranging his teaching across five long discourses through the account that followed, five blocks matching, more than one reader over the centuries has noticed, the five books Moses himself was said to have written. It closed with instruction to go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, language some scholars read as reflecting how the community's own worship had already come to speak by the time this account was finally written down, more than the historical man's own words that day, word for word.

III. What the Physician Investigated

A third writer opened by admitting outright that he was not the first to attempt this. Many, he wrote, have undertaken to compile a narrative of the things accomplished among us, and having investigated everything carefully from the beginning, it seemed good to me also to write an orderly account, so that his reader might know the certainty of what he had been taught. It was the closest any of these four accounts came to describing its own working method out loud, in language any educated reader of that century would have recognized as the standard opening move of a historian, not a claim of modern verification but a real, deliberate claim of careful compiling all the same.

His version of the birth kept none of the magi, none of Herod's slaughter, none of the flight to Egypt. A young woman named Mary, told she would bear a son though she had known no man, answered simply, let it be to me according to your word, and went to see her older kinswoman Elizabeth, herself unexpectedly pregnant, and broke into a song that echoed, almost line for line in places, a song sung centuries earlier by another woman who had once been barren and had been lifted, in her own turn, out of shame into honor. My soul magnifies the Lord, Mary sang, he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate, he has filled the hungry with good things and sent the rich away empty. The child was born not among wise men from the east but among shepherds, ordinary night watchmen out with their flocks, the first people in this account to hear the news at all.

Where the earlier writer's mountain had opened, blessed are the poor in spirit, this one's opened plainer and harder to soften into anything but what it said outright: blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation already. The same teaching, remembered or shaped differently by two different communities, and the two versions do not quietly agree about exactly what had been promised to whom.

His account of the risen man, when it came, held nothing of the first writer's terrified silence. Two disciples walking the road to Emmaus, grieving, were joined by a stranger who walked with them and opened the scriptures to them, beginning with Moses and all the prophets, and it was only over supper, when he took bread and blessed it and broke it, that their eyes were opened and they recognized him, and he vanished from their sight the same instant they finally knew who he was.

He wrote a second scroll after the first, picking up where it left off. Not many days later the whole community gathered in Jerusalem was filled, all at once, with what felt like wind and fire, and began speaking in languages not their own, and the crowd that gathered outside, pilgrims from a dozen different nations, each heard the message in his own native tongue, a single morning undoing, for anyone paying attention to the old story, exactly what one particular tower had once scattered into confusion. Not long after, a man named Stephen was stoned to death for preaching too openly, and the persecution that followed his death scattered the believers out of Jerusalem entirely, into the countryside, and beyond it, further than any of them had yet gone.

IV. The Ethiopian's Road, and the Council's Second Telling

One of those scattered, a man named Philip, was told by an angel to take the desert road running south from Jerusalem toward Gaza, with no further explanation given him than that. On it he found a chariot carrying a court official of considerable rank, in charge of the entire treasury of the kandake, the title carried by the ruling queens of Kush, a kingdom old already by then, governed from Meroë far up the Nile, in what is now Sudan. He had come that whole distance to Jerusalem to worship, and was reading, on his way home, from the scroll of the prophet Isaiah, sounding the words out loud as travelers of that century often did.

Philip ran alongside the chariot and asked if he understood what he was reading. He did not, and said so, and asked how he could unless someone guided him, and invited Philip up to sit beside him. The passage under his eye was one that had been argued over for centuries already by the time either man was born: like a sheep led to the slaughter, and like a lamb before its shearer is silent, so he opens not his mouth. Who is the prophet talking about, the official asked, himself, or someone else. Philip told him it was Jesus. As the chariot rolled on, they came to water, and the official, without waiting for any further teaching or any period of preparation, asked the one direct question the moment allowed: what is to prevent me from being baptized. Nothing did. He went down into the water with Philip and came up again and continued on his own road home rejoicing, while Philip was carried off elsewhere by the same spirit that had sent him out in the first place, never to see the man again.

Kush was its own old kingdom, ruled from its own city, answering to no one else's throne. It was not the highland kingdom of Aksum, three centuries and several hundred miles removed, which would not adopt this faith as its own official religion until long after this chariot had already gone home to Meroë, and whose church would go on to keep Enoch's book whole in its own script for two thousand years after everyone else had let it go. The Nile linked them loosely, and nothing else did; neither kingdom owed the other anything for what happened here, or for what would happen at Aksum's own court three centuries later. What they shared was only this: each found its own way into the faith without needing Rome, or Europe, or anyone else's mission, to bring it to them first.

No council had been convened to decide whether a foreign court official, a eunuch, a man excluded by an older law from full assembly in Israel's own worship, belonged inside this new movement. No formal debate weighed his case at all. He read scripture, asked a question, was answered honestly, and was welcomed on the spot, on a desert road, witnessed by no one but the two of them.

A council did convene, not much later, over a related and much harder-fought question: whether Gentile believers generally needed circumcision to belong. In this second writer's telling, the meeting in Jerusalem went smoothly enough. Peter stood and reminded the assembly that God had shown no partiality between Jew and Gentile, and asked why they would now place on new believers a yoke neither their own fathers nor they themselves had ever been able to bear. James proposed a modest compromise, abstinence from food offered to idols, from sexual immorality, from meat of strangled animals and from blood, and the whole assembly agreed, and a letter went out carrying the decision, and the matter, in this account, closed there, settled and at peace.

It had not felt that settled to the man who had actually stood in the middle of it years earlier and written about it himself, in a letter this project has already read in full. Paul's own account of roughly the same dispute, written far closer to the events than this later, calmer retelling, described Peter caught, in the moment, doing exactly the opposite of what he stands up here to argue for, withdrawing from a shared table out of fear the instant certain men from James actually arrived, and being publicly opposed for it, to his face, by Paul himself. Most scholars weigh Paul's own contemporary letter as the more reliable witness to how contested this fight actually was while it was still being fought, precisely because a later, calmer narrative, composed once the outcome was already settled and the community had made its peace with it, tends to smooth exactly the roughness a witness closer to the argument still remembers clearly. One fight, over who belonged and on what terms, remembered twice: once written in the heat of it, once written after the heat had passed. Neither telling lied, exactly. They simply stood at two very different distances from the same flame.

V. The Word Before All Things

The last of the four accounts to be written opened with no genealogy, no birth, no baptism scene at all, but with a claim reaching back further than any of the other three had dared to reach: in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made. It was not the first time this project has encountered a divine Word or Wisdom described this way, present at creation, active in shaping everything that followed. A Jewish teacher in Alexandria, a century or so earlier, had written of Wisdom herself present at the world's beginning in nearly identical terms. This writer, working in a different city, a different generation, and a different language of devotion entirely, took that same inherited idea and did something no one before him had done with it: named it directly, without qualification, as this one man.

He kept almost none of the material the earlier three accounts shared with each other. In his telling, Jesus met a Samaritan woman alone at a well at noon and asked her for water, a request that crossed two boundaries at once by the ordinary reckoning of that world, a Jewish man speaking openly to a woman not his kin, and a Jew accepting water from a Samaritan vessel at all, peoples who shared history and scripture and centuries of mutual contempt in roughly equal measure. How is it, she asked him, that you, a Jew, ask a drink of me, a woman of Samaria. He answered her longer and more directly than he answered almost anyone else in this account, told her outright what he was, and she left her water jar behind at the well and ran to tell her whole town.

Later still, at the tomb of a friend named Lazarus, dead four days already, the account says Jesus wept, openly, in front of the mourners gathered there, before calling into the open tomb, Lazarus, come out, and the dead man walked out still bound in his own burial cloths.

What set this last account apart from the other three most of all was how little patience it had for the silence the earliest one had built its whole structure around. Before Abraham was, this writer's Jesus says, to a crowd that understood exactly what he was claiming by it, I am. The earliest account had built an entire narrative around the question of who this man was, answered only at the very end, and only by an outsider, at the foot of a cross. The last account skipped the question entirely and let the man answer it himself, over and over, in his own voice, from nearly the first page. Read in the order they were actually written, not the order later custom bound them into one book, the four accounts stop looking like four windows onto one settled certainty and start looking like exactly what they are: a claim that grew bolder, and more explicit, and more confidently spoken, across the thirty years it took four different communities to write it down.
