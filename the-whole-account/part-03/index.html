Part Three: What the Prophets Carried

This part follows the crisis rather than the calendar: Judah under Assyrian pressure, then Babylonian, through the fall of Jerusalem and into the exile's final years. Isaiah's material spans roughly two centuries under one name, three distinct voices critical scholarship reads as three different authors bound into one scroll; that division is treated here as established. Where a text's own meaning has been read differently by later tradition than it most plausibly meant to its first hearers, especially Isaiah's sign to Ahaz, the earlier meaning is what's dramatized. Jeremiah survives in two genuinely different ancient editions, Hebrew and Greek; Lamentations' authorship, despite old tradition, is not accepted as Jeremiah's own by critical scholarship, and is kept anonymous here accordingly.

I. The Coal and the Two Kings

Isaiah saw the vision in the year King Uzziah died, the throne room impossibly high, the hem of a robe filling the whole temple, winged creatures calling to each other until the doorposts shook. Woe is me, he said, for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell among a people of unclean lips. One of the creatures took a live coal from the altar with tongs and touched it to his mouth. Your guilt is taken away, it told him, and your sin is atoned for. When the question came, whom shall I send, he answered before he had time to weigh what answering would cost him. Here am I. Send me.

What it cost him arrived a few years later at the conduit of the upper pool, where King Ahaz stood inspecting Jerusalem's water supply with the specific dread of a king expecting a siege. Aram and the northern kingdom of Israel had allied against him, meaning to force Judah into their coalition against Assyria or replace him with a king who'd join it willingly, and Ahaz's heart, the record says, shook like the trees of the forest shake in wind. Isaiah met him there with his own small son in tow, whose name meant a remnant shall return, and told him not to be afraid of two smoking firebrands about to go out. Ask a sign of the Lord your god, he said. Ask it as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven.

Ahaz refused, in language pious enough to sound like faith. I will not put the Lord to the test. It was not faith. He had already sent to the king of Assyria, offering himself as vassal and servant, silver and gold stripped from the temple itself to pay for the alliance he actually trusted. Isaiah gave him the sign anyway, unasked: a young woman, already known to be with child, would bear a son and call him Immanuel, God with us, and before that boy was old enough to know to refuse evil and choose good, the land of the two kings Ahaz feared would lie deserted. It was a sign with a clock on it, a matter of a few years, not a promise for some distant age. Ahaz got his Assyrian alliance. He also got, within a generation, an Assyrian vassal-tax that would outlast the threat it was bought against by decades.

The son who inherited that debt, Hezekiah, stood on Jerusalem's own wall years later listening to a different foreign voice entirely: the Assyrian king's field commander, shouting up in Hebrew loud enough for the ordinary people on the wall to understand every word, past the officials sent out to negotiate quietly. Do not let Hezekiah deceive you, the commander called. On whom do you now rely, that you rebel against me? Egypt, that broken reed? Has any god of any nation ever delivered its land from the king of Assyria's hand? Say to Hezekiah, let not your god on whom you trust deceive you.

Hezekiah tore his clothes and went up to the temple and sent word to Isaiah. The prophet's answer this time carried none of his father's evasions in it to work against: the Assyrian king would hear a rumor, and return to his own land, and fall there by the sword. In the morning, the record says, the Assyrian camp was full of corpses, and the siege lifted. What actually turned that army back, whether disease or a threat elsewhere in the empire or something this account no longer preserves clearly enough to say, isn't something history can currently settle with confidence. What can be said is simpler: one king, faced with an empire's pressure, bought a human patron and paid for it for a generation. His son, faced with the very empire his father had leaned on, chose the other patron available to him, and the siege that should have ended Jerusalem did not.

II. The Scroll and the Fire

Jeremiah stood at the temple gate on a feast day, when the crowds moving through were thickest, and told them the thing least welcome to hear in that particular doorway: do not trust in these deceptive words, this is the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, as though saying it three times made the building itself a wall nothing could breach. Go now to Shiloh, he told them, where God's name once dwelt too, and see what became of that place for the wickedness of my people. Amend your ways, or I will do to this house as I did to Shiloh.

The priests and the prophets and the crowd around him did not hear a warning. They heard a man threatening the temple's destruction inside the temple's own gate, and seized him. You shall die, they told him, and meant it clearly enough that officials had to come down from the palace and hold a hearing at the gate itself before a mob decided the question for them. An elder in the crowd remembered aloud that a prophet named Micah had said something nearly as harsh under a king two generations dead, and had not been killed for it, and the memory bought Jeremiah his life that day. It did not buy the same mercy for a man named Uriah, who had prophesied the same warning around the same time and fled to Egypt when the king wanted him dead for it; the king sent men after him, brought him back, and had him killed anyway, his body thrown into a common burial ground, a plain reminder to everyone watching Jeremiah's own trial of exactly how this could have gone.

Years later, barred by then from entering the temple himself, Jeremiah dictated the whole of his message across more than twenty years to a scribe named Baruch, who wrote it on a scroll a column at a time, and sent Baruch to read it aloud in the temple in his place. Officials who heard it reported it up to the king's own house, and Baruch was summoned to read it again, to a smaller and more frightened room, and this time the men listening told him to go into hiding, and to take Jeremiah with him.

The scroll itself went up to King Jehoiakim, in his winter apartment, a fire burning in the brazier in front of him against the season's cold. As it was read to him, column by column, the king took a scribe's small knife and cut each finished section free and dropped it into the fire, and kept doing this until the whole scroll had gone into the flames, and neither he nor anyone in the room with him tore his clothes, the ordinary gesture of alarm at hearing something this dire, the way Hezekiah had torn his at the siege wall a century earlier. A king burning a prophet's words column by column, unmoved, was its own kind of answer to the warning itself.

Jeremiah, safely hidden, had Baruch take up a fresh scroll and dictate the whole thing again, from memory, and the record notes that many similar words were added to it besides. The king had burned one copy. What survived him was not that copy, but a second version, already different from the first before either had left the room where it was written.

III. Salt and Bone

The exiles at Tel-abib, beside the Chebar canal in Babylon, had a priest among them named Ezekiel who had stopped, for a long stretch of time, behaving like a man they recognized. He lay bound on one side for a great many days, then the other, a single posture held for what felt to the households nearby like most of a year, a living model of the siege Jerusalem was still enduring without him. He baked his bread over dung fires, the fuel of a besieged city's last resort, and only balked, the one place in the whole performance where the man himself pushed back against what he'd been told to do, at using human waste for the fire; he was allowed cow dung instead, a small mercy inside an otherwise merciless sign. Later he took a sword to his own head and beard, shaved them both, and divided the hair three ways in front of anyone watching: a third burned in the city's model at the center of the sign, a third struck with the sword's edge, a third scattered to the wind, a few strands kept back and bound in his robe, a smaller number of those thrown into the fire after. The exiles who watched him do this over months did not, by and large, think they were watching a message. Most people watching a priest behave this way in front of them think they are watching a man come apart.

What he showed them later, in the valley, was different in kind. Bones without number, dry, scattered across the ground, and the question put to him directly: son of man, can these bones live? He gave the only honest answer available to a man being asked to judge God's own power. O Lord, you know. He prophesied to the bones as instructed, and heard, he said, a rattling, bone finding bone, sinews and flesh coming up over them, skin closing over that, and no breath in them yet until he prophesied a second time to the breath itself, from the four winds, and the bodies stood on their feet, a vast army. He did not let anyone listening mistake what they had just heard for a promise that the individually dead would rise from their own graves. These bones, he told them, are the whole house of Israel. Our bones are dried up, our hope is lost, we are indeed cut off, is what the exiles themselves had been saying. What he had just shown them, in his own explanation of it, was a nation given breath again, not one man walking out of a tomb. Whatever came later out of that image, in later centuries and later religions entirely, this was a vision about a whole scattered people, coming home.

IV. What the Alphabet Could Not Hold

Someone who survived the siege sat down afterward and counted through the wreckage the way a person counts when counting is the only order left available to them, a line for every letter, in sequence, because grief without a shape to hold it will simply run out into nothing at all.

The city sits alone, the counting begins, that was full of people. Princes have become like deer that find no pasture, fleeing without strength before the pursuer. Young and old lie on the ground in the streets. Priests and prophets were killed in the sanctuary itself. The tongue of the nursing infant clings to the roof of its mouth for thirst; children beg for bread and no one gives it to them. The poems name, without turning away and without lingering, what hunger this total had driven some mothers to do to their own children rather than watch them starve by inches, and offer no comfort alongside the naming, only the naming itself, because comfort would have been a lie about what that winter actually was.

The middle poem, the longest, counts three lines to every letter instead of one, as though the grief closest to the center of the collection needed triple the discipline to stay inside any shape at all rather than simply breaking the speaker apart. It is there that the one widely remembered line of comfort in the whole work sits, buried inside the counting rather than announced above it: his mercies are new every morning, great is your faithfulness. It does not undo anything that came before it. It sits beside the wreckage, not instead of it.

By the final poem the counting itself gives out. Twenty-two lines still, the length the alphabet would have required, but no longer ordered letter by letter the way the earlier four had been, the pattern that had held the grief in shape for four whole poems finally letting go in the fifth, the same way a besieged city's own order gives out in the end no matter how long it holds. It closes without resolution, a question thrown at God rather than an answer received from him: turn us back to you, and we will be turned; renew our days as of old, unless you have utterly rejected us, and are angry with us beyond measure. The poems do not tell the reader which of those two endings is the true one. They stop there, mid-question, because that was where the counting actually ran out.

V. Comfort, and What Waited After

Decades into the exile, with an old generation mostly dead in Babylon and a new one grown up knowing no other home, a different voice began speaking among them, no longer indicting anyone. Comfort, comfort my people, it said, speak tenderly to Jerusalem, tell her that her time of hard service is over. It named, directly and by name, a foreign king not yet finished conquering anything: Cyrus, my shepherd, my anointed, whose right hand I have grasped, though he does not know me. No prophet before this one had ever put that title on a man outside Israel entirely, and it meant something specific to anyone listening who understood the weight the word normally carried: their god's own chosen instrument for going home again, and it was a Persian.

The same voice turned, in the next breath, to mockery of the very gods the exiles had lived among for a generation. A craftsman cuts down a tree, it said, and burns half of it to warm himself and bake his bread, and carves the other half into a god, and bows down to it, and prays, deliver me, for you are my god, apparently blind to the fact that the fire in his own hearth was cut from the same tree as the god now receiving his prayers. Against gods carved from firewood, this voice set a single claim, sharper than anything said on the subject before it: there is no other, there is no god besides me.

Woven through the same stretch of oracles were four passages about a servant, suffering, despised, bearing what wasn't his to bear, wounded for others' transgressions. Some who heard it took the servant to mean the whole scattered, suffering people itself, Israel named directly as the servant in one of the four songs. Others heard the same words and thought of one man in particular, though which man, or whether one man was even meant, isn't a question this account or the prophet's own words settle clearly enough to say with confidence. What can be said honestly is only that the ambiguity was there from the start, for the very first people who heard it sung, not invented later by readers looking for a doctrine to fit onto it.

Cyrus came, and Babylon fell, and the exiles did go home, and what waited for them there was not the return the comfort oracles had promised. The city was smaller than memory had kept it, poorer, the walls still broken, and a new voice, later than the first two, had to speak into the actual disappointment of people who had crossed the desert on the strength of comfort and arrived to find comfort was not the same thing as restoration. This voice kept faith with the older concern for justice even here, fasting without mercy toward the poor condemned in the same breath as festival worship. And it opened a door the returning community itself was already beginning to argue about closing: let not the foreigner who has joined himself to the Lord say, the Lord will surely separate me from his people, and let not the eunuch say, I am a dry tree, for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples. Whether that door would stay open was not a question this voice could settle by itself. It would be argued out, hard, by the very people now walking back through it.
