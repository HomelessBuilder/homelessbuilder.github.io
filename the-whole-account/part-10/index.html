Part Ten: The Closing Vision

Revelation draws so heavily on Daniel's imagery and structure that discussing it responsibly requires Daniel's own material first, even though Daniel was never given its own place earlier in this project; it's included here, at the point it's actually needed, rather than left as a gap. Revelation's own date is genuinely contested between the mid-60s CE, under Nero, and the mid-90s, under Domitian, both with real scholarly support; its authorship is widely held distinct from the Gospel and letters bearing John's name, a doubt raised as early as the third century, not a modern imposition. The Nero reading of 666 is among the stronger proposed solutions to that number, not a settled one, out of a genuinely enormous interpretive history. And the older picture of severe, empire-wide persecution specifically under Domitian has been significantly revised by more recent historians toward something more sporadic and local; that revision doesn't settle the dating question, only means the vision's fury shouldn't be read as automatic proof of one particular emperor's active campaign against the faith.

I. What the Vision Already Knew

Daniel and three companions, taken young into the Babylonian court and offered the same rich food and wine served to the king's own household, asked instead for vegetables and water, and after ten days stood visibly healthier than every young man who had eaten as the king intended. It was a small refusal, made without confrontation, inside a system they had no power to leave and no wish to sabotage. They served that court faithfully for years afterward. They simply would not eat what would have quietly unmade them while they did it.

Years later, three of them stood before a golden image set up on the plain of Dura and would not bow when the music sounded, though every other official in the province did. If our God is able to deliver us, they told the furious king, he will deliver us, and if not, be it known to you that we will not serve your gods. The furnace was stoked seven times hotter than usual, hot enough to kill the men who threw them in, and the king, watching, saw not three men in the flames but four, walking freely, unburned, the fourth like nothing born of that world at all.

A different king, generations on, drank from the temple's own captured vessels at a private feast, and a hand appeared without a body attached to it and wrote four words on the plaster wall that no wise man in the room could read. Daniel, called in as a last resort, read it without flinching: your kingdom has been weighed and found wanting, and is being given to others. That same night the king was killed, and the kingdom passed to Persia before morning.

Daniel himself, older now and trusted enough to draw the envy of rivals, was caught keeping to his old habit of praying toward Jerusalem three times a day at his open window, in open defiance of a decree drawn up specifically to trap him in it. He was thrown to the lions for it and found untouched in the morning, and the men who had engineered the trap were thrown in in his place and never reached the floor of the den alive.

The visions given to him directly, later in his life, moved past kings and courts entirely. Four beasts rose out of a churning sea, one after another, each stranger than the last, each standing for an empire that would rise and pass in its turn. Among them, coming with the clouds of heaven, was one like a son of man, brought before the Ancient of Days and given dominion and glory and a kingdom that would never pass away or be destroyed, language this project has already heard once before, spoken by a different visionary, in a text most of Daniel's later readers never held as scripture at all.

A final vision tracked the wars between two rival dynasties with a precision that reads less like prophecy than memory, kingdom against kingdom, marriage alliances and betrayals and armies marching south and north again, growing sharper and more specific the closer it drew to one particular king's own persecution and the desecration of the temple itself. And then, at the exact point where that king's own death should have come, the vision's precision simply gave out, vague where everything before it had been exact, the unmistakable seam where a writer looking backward at real events runs out of real events to describe and has to guess, for the first time, what comes next. It closed on a promise the earlier prophets in this project's whole account had never stated quite this openly: many who sleep in the dust of the earth shall wake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. Not a nation restored, this time, but the dead themselves, judged one by one.

II. Letters to Seven Lamps

A prophet named John, on the island of Patmos, was in the spirit on the Lord's own day when a voice behind him, loud as a trumpet, told him to write what he was about to see and send it to seven churches scattered through the province of Asia.

He wrote each of them their own letter first, before anything else, and none of the seven got the same one. Ephesus was praised for its patient endurance and told, in the same breath, that it had abandoned the love it once had, and needed to remember, and return. Smyrna, materially poor and facing suffering the letter did not pretend it could prevent, was told, I know your poverty, but you are rich, and promised a crown for whoever stayed faithful even to death. Laodicea, comfortable, self-satisfied, sure of its own wealth, received the sharpest line of all seven: because you are lukewarm, and neither cold nor hot, I will spit you out of my mouth. You say I am rich and need nothing, and you do not realize that you are wretched, and offered, instead of the gold it already had, gold refined by fire, the only kind actually worth having.

Once the letters were finished, the vision opened onto something larger. A door stood open in heaven, and a throne circled by a rainbow, and living creatures and elders casting their own crowns down before it, and in the hand of the one seated there, a scroll sealed shut seven times over, which no one anywhere, in heaven or on earth or under it, was found worthy to open. John wept at that, openly, until one of the elders told him to stop: weep no more, the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered, he is able to open the scroll. John turned to see the Lion he'd just been promised, and what stood there instead was a Lamb, marked as though it had been slaughtered, and every voice in that entire vision, without exception, broke into worship due to God alone, and gave it to the Lamb instead.

III. Seals, and the Two Beasts

The seals broke open one after another. Four riders came out on horses of different colors, one bringing conquest, one war, one famine, one death, and behind them the sun went dark and the moon turned the color of blood and the stars fell like fruit shaken loose from a tree in a high wind. Whether these sequences of seven, seals and then trumpets and then bowls, describe one future unfolding step by step, or several visions of the same underlying reality seen again and again from different angles, is a real, live disagreement among the people who study this text closely, not a question this account settles either way.

A woman appeared next, clothed with the sun, the moon under her feet, crying out in the pain of labor while a great red dragon waited in front of her, ready to devour the child the instant it was born. The child was caught up to God's own throne before the dragon could reach it, and the dragon, thrown down out of heaven entirely, turned its fury on the woman instead, who fled into the wilderness to a place prepared for her, kept there, out of its reach, for a time.

Two beasts followed the dragon's fall. One rose out of the sea, given the dragon's own authority, and the whole world worshipped it, marveling that a wound that should have killed it hadn't. A second beast served the first, working wonders in its name, forcing everyone, small and great, rich and poor, free and slave, to carry a mark before they could buy or sell anything at all. Its number, the vision said, was the number of a man, and anyone who had understanding could work it out, six hundred and sixty-six. Any reader of that century who took a certain recently dead emperor's own name and added up the numerical value of its letters, in the way readers of that world did for names all the time, would have arrived at that exact figure, or close enough to it that a slightly different spelling produced the alternate number a few surviving copies of this vision give instead. It is not the only guess history has ever made about that number. It remains among the better grounded of a very long list.

IV. Babylon Falls, and What Comes After

A great city appeared next, seated on many waters, dressed in purple and scarlet, drunk on the blood of everyone who had stood against her. Merchants wept over her fall, not for grief at what she'd done but for the cargo they could no longer sell there, gold and silk and spice and ivory, and, near the very bottom of the list, human bodies and souls, named as merchandise beside everything else. Almost no serious reader of this vision doubts, in the end, whose city was actually meant by a name borrowed from an empire that had already fallen centuries before this one was written, a code other Jewish writers of the same rough period used for the identical purpose.

What followed moved fast toward an ending: the beast and the deceiver defeated, a thousand years of reign, a final loosing and a final defeat of everything still opposed to God, material that would go on to fuel argument among later Christians for as long as Christians have argued about anything, long after this project's own timeline runs out.

What came last was not judgment at all. A new heaven, a new earth, the sea itself simply gone, and a city descending like a bride prepared for her husband, and a voice from the throne promising what the account had been building toward since its very first line: he will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away. A river ran from the throne through the middle of the city, clear as crystal, and on either side of it grew a tree bearing fruit in every season, its leaves given, the vision said, for the healing of the nations. Not one nation. All of them, without exception, the same word this whole account has kept circling back to since the very first oldest poem it ever held: who belongs, and on what terms, and to whom the healing is finally offered.

It had begun, at the very start of this whole undertaking, with a handful of poems old enough that no king or temple or settled theology existed yet to shape them, a storm-god fighting for scattered tribes who had to show up and be counted to belong to each other at all. It closes here, centuries and empires and arguments later, with a tree whose leaves are offered, without a single boundary drawn around who may come and take them, to every nation the whole account had ever named.
